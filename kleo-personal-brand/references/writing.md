# Voice, ghostwriting, editing and repurposing

## Build a usable voice guide

Use client-authored material or drafts explicitly approved as representative. Prefer several samples across contexts. Distinguish the author's writing from marketing copy written by someone else, interview questions, quoted passages and competitor inspiration.

For each pattern, retain a short example reference and confidence. Record conversational vs formal tone, pacing, vocabulary/technical density, paragraph rhythm, directness, use of humor, first-person stance, openings, transitions, endings, emoji/hashtag habits and explicitly forbidden phrases. A preference is strongest when the client states it or approves it repeatedly.

Three samples are enough to begin calibration, not prove perfect mimicry. With sparse evidence, use a provisional voice and a short calibration draft. Do not impose generic “short punchy lines” on an author whose samples are thoughtful prose. Preserve recognizable phrasing where appropriate, while correcting avoidable ambiguity.

If samples and instructions conflict, current explicit client preferences win. Do not copy the style of a different client. Translate inspiration references into useful general traits while keeping the client's own identity central; reference material never supplies the client's personal history.

## Brief every post internally

Before writing, resolve:

- Client and post ID; publication language.
- Specific reader and their current question/problem.
- One intended change in understanding or action.
- One original insight or useful explanation.
- Source IDs and usable facts, including private-use restrictions.
- Pillar, purpose and suitable format.
- Relevant active voice rules and approved examples.
- CTA, if one serves the purpose; no CTA is also valid.

If the only available material is a topic, find a defensible angle through research or an interview. Do not manufacture a first-person event to make the post compelling.

## Drafting sequence

1. Choose the central idea and the evidence that supports it.
2. Consider a few different openings internally: clear observation, specific problem, counterpoint, useful question, or evidenced result. Select the one that fits the author's voice and the actual body. Do not expose ten hooks unless asked.
3. Develop the idea with reasoning, a demonstration, authentic story, comparison or actionable method. Every paragraph should advance the promise.
4. End with a concrete takeaway, an earned question, or a relevant next step. Avoid an unrelated sales pitch or engagement bait.
5. Check the public copy separately from research notes, then run the quality gate.

A hook is a promise of relevance. Never make it stronger than the evidence. “I lost $50,000” needs a true, authorized client story. “Three checks before you automate follow-ups” requires three useful checks, not a fake result. Curiosity should not depend on hiding essential context.

## Adaptable structures (original tools, not fixed Kleo formulas)

| Structure | Useful for | Development |
|---|---|---|
| Observed problem | Shared audience friction | Specific situation → cause → better choice → implication |
| Teaching | Demonstrating expertise | Reader question → explanation → example/checklist → application |
| Decision/tradeoff | Differentiation | Two plausible options → when each fits → author's reasoning |
| Real story | Connection and learning | Actual moment → decision → consequence → transferable lesson |
| Case study | Proof | Baseline/scope → intervention → evidenced outcome → caveat/lesson |
| Point of view | Recognizable perspective | Claim → reasoning/evidence → fair counterargument → conclusion |
| Build in public | Showing work | Current problem → what was tried → what was learned → next test |
| Offer fit | Useful conversion | Audience situation → who benefits → mechanism/proof → real next step |

Choose structure after the idea; do not force every post into “hook → three bullets → question.” A post can be concise or developed according to the material and author. Verify current platform limits when preparing a live post rather than relying on stale hardcoded lengths.

## Evidence and first-person checks

Every number, named customer, quotation, credential, unusual result, product claim and first-person event must map to client-matched evidence. Preserve units, timeframe and limitations. A scenario can be clearly hypothetical; never make it look like an actual client's project. Do not convert correlation into causation or a pilot result into a general promise.

Use direct source attribution where factual reporting needs it. Internal provenance can stay outside a personal post, but readers must not be misled about external statistics or quoted ideas. Avoid uncredited copying of distinctive expression from swipe files.

For unverified personal claims, remove the claim or ask for evidence. Do not “fix” a fabricated result merely by removing the number while keeping the invented event. For example, change an unsupported client-success story into a clearly framed decision guide, not “a client saw major improvement.”

For medical, legal, financial or similarly consequential domains, verify specific claims with appropriate current primary sources and preserve the author's actual qualifications. Do not invent individual advice or certainty for persuasive effect.

## Editorial passes

Pass 1 — substance: Is the idea useful and specific? Does the body deliver the hook? Remove unsupported detail, unnecessary claims and duplicated conclusions.

Pass 2 — voice: Read it against the client's guide and examples. Replace generic consultant phrasing with the client's level of precision, rhythm and perspective. Eliminate boilerplate such as “in today's fast-paced world,” empty superlatives and canned contrast constructions unless the client actually prefers them.

Pass 3 — reading experience: Check the opening, logical flow, line breaks, mobile readability, accessible formatting, grammar, CTA and exact requested length/format. Do not use decorative Unicode bold or artificial one-word lines by default. Respect the author's normal style.

For a news-led LinkedIn post, build around one reader-relevant implication rather than narrating the research process. Keep the attribution and uncertainty needed to interpret a claim, but move calculation workings, source-access failures and repeated caveats into editorial notes. For example, identify a proposed cause as an analyst explanation in the public copy; explain missing datasets in the notes unless their absence changes the post's central claim. Prefer a small number of connected beats over a paragraph for every verified fact. Judge readability separately from factual completeness: a fully sourced draft can still need substantial compression.

Finish earlier if all gates pass. If a pass exposes missing source material, mark the affected draft `needs-facts` or replace the unsupported angle. Never label unfinished placeholders ready to publish.

## Repurposing a transcript or long-form source

Extract a set of distinct insights, not a set of paraphrases. Track speaker, page/timestamp and permission for each. Separate the client's own words from guests, questions and quotations. Choose different audience problems/purposes: explanation, tradeoff, misconception, authentic lesson, practical process or evidenced result. Do not repeatedly recycle the same conclusion as a “new” post.

Write each post so it stands alone without claiming events absent from the source. Preserve uncertainty and context. If adapting for another platform, change packaging to that destination and verify limits; do not promise multi-platform scheduling merely because drafting is possible.

## Deliverable format

For one post, give the final clean draft with a short note only if the user needs to choose or verify something. For a batch, give an ID/title, purpose or schedule slot when useful, and the full body for every requested post. Source/quality notes belong outside the public copy. Keep necessary source attribution natural in the post; put supporting clickable references beside the draft or in its source notes when the destination does not render Markdown links. Include URLs in the post when the brief or reader's next action calls for them. Provide visual direction or alternative hooks only when requested or materially useful.

After client edits, learn the durable preference if indicated, keep one-off changes local, and save the approved version. An unchanged draft is not automatically an approved voice sample or permission to publish.
